# Test understanding, not just clarity, in money copy

Excellent UK consumer finance copy is copy that a tested reader can recall and act on, not copy that looks plain. The FCA's March 2026 consumer understanding review describes good practice as **plain language, key information upfront, layered detail, risks given "equal prominence to benefits", and comprehension testing before and after changes**, with one firm redrafting until vulnerable customers hit **at least 80% correct recall of key points**. GOV.UK supplies the most concrete house rules (a **25-word sentence limit**, numerals, "£75.50" not "£75.00", "4 June 2017", sentence case, error messages that state the problem and the fix). The audience case is strong: **18% of adults in England are at Level 1 or below in literacy and 21% in numeracy**, and in government debt-letter trials **at most 35%** of readers understood the originals while **at least 60% claimed to**. Behavioural research adds that people linearise compound growth, anchor on minimum payments and avoid threatening financial information. Several popular "facts" do not survive checking: GOV.UK's "reading age 9" target and the American Press Institute sentence-length figures have no verified primary source, and FCA Handbook rule text could not be read directly.

## The Consumer Duty makes comprehension a measurable outcome

The consumer understanding rule asks that communications meet retail customers' information needs, are likely to be understood, and **"equip retail customers to make decisions that are effective, timely and properly informed"**, alongside a "clear, fair and not misleading" limb (QUOTED-VIA, [PRIN 2A.5](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PRIN/2A/5.html); [FTN glossary](https://skills.fintechnorth.uk/mod/glossary/showentry.php?eid=456)). A search summary of the rule text adds **"plain and intelligible language"** and, where jargon is unavoidable, explaining "the meaning of any jargon or technical terms as simply as possible" (QUOTED-VIA, paragraph number not confirmed).

The clearest statement of what good looks like is the FCA's March 2026 review (VERIFIED, [FCA good practice](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/good-and-poor-practice/consumer-understanding-good-practice-areas-improvement)). Effective firms use **"plain language, clear structure, visual hierarchy and layered content"**, "place essential information upfront", "avoid jargon, use clear summaries" and **"give risks equal prominence to benefits"**. They test "both before and after changes", and one firm redrafted until vulnerable customers reached **at least 80% correct recall of key points**. Poor practice is equally specific: changing wording, icons or colours "without improving clarity, sequencing or prominence of key information", **risk warnings "placed at the end of long mobile journeys"**, offers highlighted "while hiding limits or risks", testing that is "superficial, one-off or poorly documented" on "very small, unrepresentative groups", and "the same design and format to all customers". Layering is endorsed but not a free pass: a QUOTED-VIA snippet reports one Open Banking test where layering cut understanding by 4% ([Open Banking](https://standards.openbanking.org.uk/?p=5397)).

Vulnerability guidance FG21/1 "remains appropriate and helpful" alongside the Duty and counts **"poor literacy or numeracy skills"** as low capability (UNVERIFIED search summary, [FCA FG21/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/finalised-guidance/guidance-firms-fair-treatment-vulnerable-customers)). The 2026 review also flags copy left untested for accessibility, language or lower financial capability (VERIFIED, same FCA page).

**Implications for our copy**

- Put the key figure and its limits on the same screen, first.
- Define every unavoidable term at first use, in one plain sentence.
- Layer detail behind the summary, and test that layering helps.
- Test high-stakes screens for recall of key points (80% is one firm's bar, not an FCA rule), including readers with low literacy or numeracy.
- Never move a risk or limit to a later step of the journey.

## "Clear, fair and not misleading" means balance and prominence

COBS 4.2.1R(1) requires communications to be **"fair, clear and not misleading"**; promotions must "give a balanced impression of the benefits and risks" and must not **"disguise, diminish or obscure important information or warnings"** (QUOTED-VIA, [FCA risk warnings](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/risk-warnings-mainstream-investments)). Cash comparisons must be "meaningful and presented in a fair and balanced way", and copy should make clear if capital is at risk (same page). FG24/1 applies prominence media-neutrally: warnings visible from the start, not truncated, not outside the main body and not behind a click (UNVERIFIED summary, [FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/en/perspectives/2024/04/fca-finalised-guidance-on-financial-promotions-on-social-media)).

For non-FCA marketing, CAP rule 3.1 says communications "must not materially mislead", 3.3 bars omitting material information, and 3.23 to 3.25 restrict "free" (QUOTED-VIA, [CAP Code section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html)).

The advice boundary matters for an information product. Generic advice is "advice or information that does not relate to a particular investment"; a personal recommendation is **"presented as suitable for the person"** or "based on a consideration of the circumstances of that person" (QUOTED-VIA, [FCA automated advice](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/innovation/innovation-pathways/automated-advice-rules-guidance)). PERG 8.28 treats advice as needing an element of opinion, with information being statements of fact or figures (QUOTED-VIA search summary, [PERG 8.28](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/8/28.html)). Targeted support, live since **6 April 2026**, is a permissioned activity offering "suggestions designed for groups of consumers with common characteristics" ([FCA PS25/22](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/policy-statements/ps25-22-supporting-consumers-pensions-investment-decisions-rules-targeted-support/printable/print)). The notes found **no FCA list of banned words**.

**Implications for our copy**

- Give risks and limits the same size, position and screen as benefits.
- Never truncate a warning or hide it behind a tap.
- Describe outcomes as facts and figures ("this shows", "if you paid £X, you would clear it by June 2028"), not opinions ("you should", "best for you").
- Do not call anything "free" unless it costs nothing beyond unavoidable delivery.
- Send any copy that segments users and suggests actions to compliance review.

## GOV.UK gives the most concrete house rules

The GDS limit of **25 words per sentence** is real: the Service Design Manual said "sentences longer than 25 words aren't accessible" (VERIFIED, [GDS blog](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2014/08/04/sentence-length-why-25-words-is-our-limit/)). The A to Z guide states "Plain English is mandatory for all of GOV.UK", lists words to avoid (such as "utilise") and warns against metaphors like "hub" (VERIFIED, [A to Z](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/)).

The same guide sets number and money style (VERIFIED, same URL): numerals for 2 and above, a comma over 999, **£ with pence only when there are pence (£75.50, not £75.00)**, "million" and "billion" in full, **"4 June 2017"** with no ordinal, "to" for ranges, **"50%" with a numeral**, "per cent" in words, rounding up to 2 decimal places. It mandates **sentence case everywhere**, bans block capitals for large amounts of text, avoids negative contractions ("cannot", not "can't"), uses "select" not "click", and uses no bold or italics for emphasis. The NHS service manual agrees on numerals and full month names, and prefers **"1 in 7 people" to "14.3%"** and "half" to "50%" where clearer ([NHS](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/numbers-measurements-dates-time)).

The Design System's error rules are explicit ([GOV.UK error message](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message/)): say what went wrong and how to fix it; reuse the question's wording; use an instruction for empty fields ("Enter your first name"); avoid "please", "sorry", "valid", "invalid", "oops" and blame words like "you forgot"; never clear fields. Errors are only for input problems. Hint text is "a single short sentence, without any full stops", and placeholder text must not replace a label (VERIFIED, [Text input](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/text-input/)).
**Implications for our copy**

- Cap sentences at 25 words.
- Write money as £1,250 or £75.50; never show £75.00.
- Write dates as 2 June 2026 and ranges with "to".
- Use sentence case for every heading, button and title.
- Errors: state the problem and the fix in the field's own words; no "please", "sorry", "oops", "invalid".
- Labels always visible; hints one short sentence, no full stop.

## Readability research: aim short, and distrust the famous numbers

NN/g recommends **sentences under 15 to 20 words** and **grade 6 to 8** for general audiences, and found that experts in science, technology and medicine also preferred concise, scannable text and rejected unfamiliar branded terms ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/)). Its eyetracking of **232 users** found an F-shaped scan in which people read more of the first paragraph than the second and rarely read word by word ([NN/g F-pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/)).

Two widely repeated figures are weaker than they look. The GDS blog cites Ann Wylie's research that readers understand **"more than 90%" at 14 words and below 10% at 43 words** (VERIFIED that GDS says this), but the parallel American Press Institute figures have **no primary study** that the notes could find (UNVERIFIED, [PRSA](https://prsay.prsa.org/2009/01/14/how-to-make-your-copy-more-readable-make-sentences-shorter/); [Wylie Communications](https://wyliecomm.com/2022/07/why-use-short-sentences)). "Reading age 9" is also shaky: the only primary GDS line is a 2016 guest post saying "it's been reported that the average reading age is 9 years old (15 would be the maximum)", which does not set a GOV.UK target (VERIFIED, [GDS blog 2016](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2016/04/06/guest-post-looking-at-the-different-ways-to-test-content/)). Plain English Campaign material could not be retrieved.

The audience data is solid. PIAAC 2023 found **18% of adults in England at Level 1 or below in literacy and 21% in numeracy**, with 14% at the lowest levels in all three domains ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/survey-of-adults-skills-2023-country-notes_ab4f6b8c-en/united-kingdom_02bc78e4-en.html); [NFER](https://www.nfer.ac.uk/publications/survey-of-adult-skills-2023-piaac-national-report-for-england/)). National Numeracy's "49% have primary-school numeracy" comes from a 2011 survey with inconsistent age equivalence, so it has weak provenance ([National Numeracy](https://nationalnumeracy.org.uk/research-skills-life-survey-2011)).

**Implications for our copy**

- Target an average of 15 to 20 words per sentence, hard cap 25.
- Start every heading, sentence and button with the figure or the action.
- Write for the 1 in 5 adults with low literacy or numeracy, including expert users.
- Cite "reading age 9" and the API percentages only as direction, never as sourced targets.

## Shame and anxiety push people away from their own numbers

The money-worry baseline is high: in MaPS's 2021 survey (n=10,306), **36% worried when thinking about their finances** and 45% were not confident managing money day to day ([MaPS](https://maps.org.uk/2022/10/10/uk-adult-financial-wellbeing-survey-2021-mental-health-report/)). FCA Financial Lives 2024 found **24% with low financial resilience** and 49% with characteristics of vulnerability ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/financial-lives/financial-lives-survey-2024-key-findings.pdf)). MMHPI argued that intimidating, capitalised statutory wording harmed people in problem debt, and the government agreed in 2020 to change default-notice rules ([MoneySavingExpert](https://blog.moneysavingexpert.com/news/2020/10/government-agrees-to-end-distressing-debt-threat-letters)); its provisional tipping point of **5 or more creditor contacts a month** is press-reported only ([Credit Connect](https://www.credit-connect.co.uk/news/over-half-people-behind-on-payments-say-theyve-had-suicidal-thoughts/)). All UNVERIFIED summaries.

The mechanism is information avoidance: information carries emotional cost, so people avoid it even when it is free and useful ([Golman et al.](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/InfoAvoidance.pdf)), though whether investors check less after bad news is contested ([Karlsson et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11166-009-9060-6); [Gherzi et al.](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/3875/1/The%20meerkat%20effect.pdf)). Social comparison can backfire: a "neighbours' average" message pushed below-average households the wrong way unless paired with an approval cue, though in energy, not finance ([Schultz et al.](https://www.scranton.edu/faculty/nolan/pdfs/social-norms-reprise_perspectives_schultz-et-al2018.pdf)).

The government debt communications toolkit is the strongest practical source ([GOV.UK toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-sector-toolkits/debt-management-communications-toolkit-v1)): lead with support, consequences second; avoid "arrears", "defaulting" and "judgement"; HMRC replaced **"you haven't paid" with "we haven't received payment"**; give exact dates; refer to **no more than two** advice agencies. It reports that **53% wait over a year** before seeking advice and **at least 1 in 5 adults have maths anxiety**. The notes found **no peer-reviewed test of shame-specific wording in a finance app**, so the rules below are inferences.

**Implications for our copy**

- State the fact, then one concrete next step and where to get help.
- Use no blame verbs ("you failed", "you missed") and no capitalised threats.
- Do not compare users with "people like you" or averages.
- Show what is under control alongside what needs attention.
- Keep prompts few; never stack reminders.

## People misread growth, minimums and certainty

Households that linearise exponential growth borrow more and save less ([Stango and Zinman](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:64:y:2009:i:6:p:2807-2849)). In UK credit card experiments, **removing the minimum payment figure stopped people choosing the minimum and raised average payments** ([FCA OP43](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-no-43-weighing-anchor-credit-card-debt)), though a real direct-debit test did not reduce card debt ([FCA note](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/research/research-note-helping-credit-card-users-repay-their-debt-summary-experimental-research/printable/print)). Natural frequencies produced up to about 50% correct Bayesian inferences versus far fewer with probabilities ([Hoffrage et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4604268)), but "1 in X" can feel more alarming than "5 in 1,000" (UNVERIFIED, [Pighin et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:sae:medema:v:31:y:2011:i:5:p:721-729)). In government Plain Numbers trials, **at most 35%** got 4 of 5 questions right on original letters, **understanding roughly doubled** after redrafting, and **at least 60% claimed to understand** the originals ([GOV.UK toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-sector-toolkits/debt-management-communications-toolkit-v1)).

On uncertainty, numeric ranges caused at most a slight drop in trust in the number and none in the source, while verbal-only caveats cost a little more ([Winton Centre](https://wintoncentre.maths.cam.ac.uk/news/being-upfront-about-your-uncertainty-doesnt-necessarily-mean-you-lose-your-audiences-trust-new-paper-published)). Words like "likely" are read inconsistently; embedding numbers improved agreement ([review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/38707974)). Bank of England work finds fan charts well understood, though the format is debated ([BoE](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty)). Pension projections commonly assume 2.5% inflation, but no study was found on whether people understand "today's money" ([PensionBee](https://www.pensionbee.com/uk/inflation-calculator)).

**Implications for our copy**

- Pair every rate with a pounds outcome ("£X after 10 years").
- Never prefill or headline a minimum payment.
- Use counts ("3 in 100") or pounds for proportions; keep % for comparable rates.
- Give projections as an estimate with a range, rounded, no pence.
- Label long-range figures "in today's money" with the inflation assumption visible.
- Test comprehension; do not ask "was this clear?".

## Fintech voice guides agree: warm voice, plain operational tone

Monzo's thread is **"straightforward kindness"**, with "warm wit" used sparingly and never in operational or legal communication; it avoids Latin-rooted business words and unexplained jargon, prefers active voice so responsibility is clear, explains impact before reasoning, says "We're sorry" once, and cites judges preferring plain English **66% to 34%** ([Monzo](https://monzo.com/tone-of-voice)). Intuit scales tone with urgency, holds that **"functional language is better than emotional language"**, names the amount, date or account ("Your credit card will expire in 30 days"), avoids "Yikes!" and keeps sensitive figures out of push notifications ([Intuit](https://contentdesign.intuit.com/voice-and-tone/)). Mailchimp keeps one voice, varies tone by the reader's state, and warns **"forced humor can be worse than none at all"** ([Mailchimp](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/)). Apple's guidance, seen only as a summary, favours specific verb buttons ("Delete Message") over "OK" ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing)). Wise, Starling and Co-op guides were not retrieved.

**Implications for our copy**

- One voice; tone shifts by situation.
- No wit, emoji or exclamation marks on balances, debt, errors or deletion.
- Name the specific amount, date and account.
- Use active voice; say "we" when we are responsible.
- Buttons repeat the verb ("Delete budget"), never "OK" or "Yes".
- No money figures in push notifications.

## Conclusion

The sources converge on one shift: the FCA no longer treats clarity as a property of the text but as a property of the reader's result. That makes recall testing, not a style checklist, the guide's real enforcement mechanism, and the Plain Numbers gap between 60% claimed and 35% actual understanding is the reason. The style rules then become the cheapest way to pass the test.

The most-quoted plain-language numbers are the least verified, and nothing tests shame-specific wording in an app. Parsing the local FG22/5 PDF and running our own comprehension tests would close the two biggest gaps.
